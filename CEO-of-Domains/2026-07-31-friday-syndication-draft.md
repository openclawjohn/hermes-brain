Howzit, Bru! A Complete Guide to South African Slang

### Body (Repurposed)

South African English is a vibrant, colourful dialect that borrows from Afrikaans, isiZulu, isiXhosa, and a dozen other languages, creating a unique linguistic tapestry that can baffle even fluent English speakers from other countries.

South African slang, often called "South African English" or simply "SA English," is more than just a collection of quirky words. It is a reflection of the country's complex history and multicultural society. From the Afrikaans-influenced "braai" to the township-born "kasi" culture, every word tells a story about the people who use it.

This guide will take you through the most common South African slang terms, their origins, and how to use them correctly. Whether you are planning a visit to Mzansi, have South African friends, or are just curious about one of the world's most expressive dialects, this guide will have you speaking like a local in no time.

### Howzit — The Universal Greeting
"Howzit" is the single most common greeting in South Africa. It is a contraction of "how is it going?" and is used in virtually every situation where you would say "hello" in other English-speaking countries. Unlike "how are you?" which expects a detailed answer, "howzit" is a greeting that requires no response other than "howzit" back.

### Braai — More Than Just a Barbecue
To an outsider, a "braai" might look like a barbecue, but to a South African, it is so much more. A braai is a social institution, a cultural ritual that brings people together around an open fire. The word comes from Afrikaans ("braaivleis" meaning grilled meat) and is used as both a noun ("we are having a braai") and a verb ("let us braai tonight").

### Eish — The All-Purpose Exclamation
"Eish" is a versatile expression of surprise, frustration, or resignation. It comes from isiZulu and isiXhosa and is used across all South African language groups. You might say "eish" when load-shedding hits, when you stub your toe, or when you hear unbelievable news.

### Lekker — The Ultimate Positive Word
"Lekker" is an Afrikaans word meaning "nice," "good," "great," or "delicious." It is used by English, Afrikaans, and Zulu speakers alike. A meal can be lekker, a party can be lekker, and a person can be lekker. It is one of the most versatile and positive words in the South African vocabulary.

### Yoh! — The Exclamation of Amazement
"Yoh!" (pronounced with a long "o" sound) is an exclamation of surprise, amazement, or shock. It comes from isiZulu ("yho!") and is used across all communities. You might say "yoh, that's expensive!" or "yoh, she's talented!"

### The Full Slang Vocabulary

**Aweh** — A greeting or expression of excitement. "Aweh, my bru!" means "Hey, my friend!"

**Bakkie** — A pickup truck. "I loaded the tools into the bakkie."

**Biltong** — Dried, cured meat, similar to beef jerky but with a distinct South African flavour profile.

**Bojwa** — Township slang for a security guard or police officer.

**Bru / Boet / Bra** — All mean "brother" or "friend." Used as terms of endearment between friends.

**Dop** — An alcoholic drink. "Let's go for a dop after work."

**Dwaal** — A daydreaming or distracted state. "Sorry, I was in a dwaal."

**Gatvol** — Fed up, had enough. "I'm gatvol of load-shedding."

**Gogga** — An insect or bug. "There's a gogga in the bathroom."

**Howzit** — The universal greeting. "Howzit, howzit" is the standard exchange.

**Isit?** — "Is that so?" or "Really?" Used as a response to news.

**Jol** — A party, good time, or fun outing. "That was a great jol last night."

**Just now / Now now** — A uniquely South African time system. "Just now" means sometime in the future (could be 5 minutes or 5 hours). "Now now" means sooner than "just now" but not immediately.

**Kasi** — A township or urban neighbourhood. "Kasi culture" refers to township lifestyle.

**Kiff** — Cool, great, awesome. "That's kiff, bru!"

**Koki** — A felt-tip pen or marker. "Pass me the red koki."

**Kop** — A cup of tea or coffee. "Let's have a quick kop."

**Kwaito** — A genre of music that originated in the townships in the 1990s.

**Larny** — Fancy, posh, or high-class. "That restaurant is too larny for me."

**Lekker** — Nice, good, great, delicious. The most versatile positive word.

**Molo** — A greeting in isiXhosa meaning "hello."

**Muti** — Traditional medicine or magic. "The sangoma gave me muti for my headache."

**Now now** — Soon, but not immediately. See "Just now" above.

**Oke** — A guy or bloke. "He's a good oke."

**Pap** — Stiff maize porridge, a staple food often served with meat and gravy.

**Robot** — Traffic light. "Turn left at the next robot."

**Sangoma** — A traditional healer or diviner.

**Sarmie** — A sandwich. "I packed a sarmie for lunch."

**Sawubona** — The isiZulu greeting meaning "I see you" — a deeply respectful way to acknowledge someone's presence.

**Schel** — A person who is mischievous or cheeky. "That kid is a real schel."

**Sharp** — "Okay," "good," "cool," or "goodbye." "Sharp sharp" means "all good" or "see you later."

**Shap shap** — Everything is fine, all good. "Don't worry, it's shap shap."

**Shongololo** — A millipede. Also used affectionately for long, slow-moving things.

**Skebenga** — A criminal or troublemaker.

**Skop** — To kick. Also used in "skop, skiet en donner" (kick, shoot, and beat) — a phrase describing rough-and-tumble action.

**Slaap** — To sleep. "I'm going to slaap now."

**Sleg** — Bad or terrible. "That movie was sleg."

**Soapie** — A soap opera. South Africans love their soapies.

**Stoep** — A veranda or porch. "Let's sit on the stoep and have a dop."

**Takkies** — Trainers or sneakers. "Put on your takkies, we're going for a run."

**Tannie** — Auntie, used respectfully for an older woman.

**Tjoppies** — Chips (french fries). "I'll have a burger with tjoppies."

**Tune** — To talk to someone, often with attitude. "Don't tune me like that!"

**Vetkoek** — A deep-fried dough bread, often filled with mince or jam.

**Voetsek** — "Go away!" or "Get lost!" — quite strong, use with caution.

**Vuvuzela** — The plastic trumpet famously blown at South African sporting events.

**Yoh!** — An exclamation of surprise or amazement.

**Ystervark** — A porcupine. Also used as a term for someone with spiky hair.

**Zef** — Cool in a trashy, retro way. Made famous by the band Die Antwoord.

### How to Speak Like a Local

The key to speaking South African English is not just knowing the words — it's understanding the rhythm and context. South Africans tend to speak with a rising intonation at the end of sentences, making statements sound like questions. The pace is relaxed, and conversations are punctuated with frequent "hey" and "neh" (right?).

When greeting, a simple "howzit" followed by a nod is standard. When saying goodbye, "sharp" or "see you" works in most situations. And if you're ever invited to a braai, the answer is always yes.
